## 1. E coli flagella movement resembles a spike

### 🔸Introduction

#### Research question

The question is whether E coli's motor signal resembles that of a spike.

#### Research gap

We ask this question because a spike is prevalent in living organism motion, both single cell (paramecium) and
multi-cellular. (Spikes are discrete signals over continuous time.) However, because the signals are of different
types (electrical vs chemical), the analogy between E coli signaling and a spiking neuron are mostly descriptive; a
quantitative comparison between the two is missing.

To make the comparison, we categorize both into the class "event", and emphasize its property of 'start and finish' and
maybe robustness.
We distinguish the two by comparing their time-scales and thresholds.

#### Importance

An event is a core concept to neuromorphic compute. We aim to add one more example to the 'event' concept, and emphasize
slow chemical signals, not just spikes, are events. In the future an event can be defined at faster/slower timescales,
composed of multiple events. All these events have patterns to them. We are looking for the patterns.

it's good.

Part I (source: Tu 2007)
$$
L(t),m(t)
\longrightarrow f_t(L,m)
\longrightarrow a(t)
\longrightarrow Y(t)$$

- $L(t)$: $\alpha$-methyl-$_{DL}$-aspartate (mM)
- $m(t)$: Mean receptor methylation
- $f_t(L, m)$: Total free-energy difference of an effective receptor cluster
- $a(t)$: Mean receptor-cluster & CheA kinase activity, $\in[0, 1]$
- $Y(t)$: Intracellular CheY-P concentration

$$f_t(m,L)=N\left[f_m(m)+f_L(L)\right],$$
where
$$
f_m(m)=\alpha(m_0-m),$$
$$
f_L(L)=
\ln\left(1+\frac{L}{K_I}\right)
-\ln\left(1+\frac{L}{K_A}\right),
$$
- $K_I, K_A$:  ligand dissociation constants of the inactive and active receptor conformations (I have no idea what this means).

$$
a=G(f_t)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(f_t)}
$$

$$
\frac{dY}{dt}=k_a a-\frac{Y}{\tau_Z}
$$
- $k_a$: Effective CheA-to-CheY phosphorylation rate
- $\tau_Z=0.5$: CheZ-controlled dephosphorylation time

Part II: feedback (source Tu et al., 2007)
**This is incomplete, no $\tilde{F}(a)$, $N, a_0, s_1, \alpha$**
$$
a(t)\longrightarrow \dot m(t)
$$
$$
\dot m(t) = F(a) = r_m\tilde{F}(a)$$, where 
$$
r_m=\frac{1}{\tau_m\alpha N a_0(1-a_0)(-s_1)}
$$
- $\tau_m=3$


Part III (source: Vlad et al., 2008)
$$
Y(t)\longrightarrow B_{CCW}(t)$$
- $B_{\mathrm{CCW}}(t)$: Counter-clockwise motor bias, $\in[0, 1]$

$$
Y_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)=\frac{\max[Y(t),0]}{Y_0}
$$
- $Y_0=3.1\ \mu\mathrm M$

$$
B_{\mathrm{CCW}}(t)=
\left[
1+
\left(\frac{1}{B_0}-1\right)
Y_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)^H
\right]^{-1},
$$
- $B_0=0.65$: adapted CCW bias
- $H=10.3$: motor Hill coefficient


#### Analysis scheme

1. Impulse response: input aspartate concentration, measure CCW bias, CheY-P levels. Purpose: show it looks like a
   spike.
2. Step response: same as above. Purpose: show it doesn't look like a spike when the input is constantly on.
3. Single E coli trace: place E coli in a 1/2D environment, measure CCW bias and CheY-P levels. Purpose: show it
   performs chemotaxis, and show in reality CheY-P doesn't look like spikes (but if you consider $\dot{CheY-P}$ to be
   fast positive feedback and $\dot{m}$ slow negative feedback, it can be similar to a spike; we can call it an "event"
   for now).
4. Population behavior: place multiple E coli on a 2D food source, observe if E coli population radiates outward like
   4G7 video. Purpose: to show this is a working model by replicating the population behavior in biology. The parameters
   in the population vary, can relate to robustness (TODO: design this experiment).
